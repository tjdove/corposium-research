# The USDC Depeg, March 2023: Background

**Status:** draft v0.1 (2026-10-13) · **Author:** Tim Dove with Claude
**Feeds:** research note §3 (calibration and validation), site page (4.3), one-pager (4.4), launch thread (4.5)
**Companion to:** [`BACKGROUND.md`](BACKGROUND.md) (Black Wednesday, 1992)

---

## Why this event is in the project

The simulator asks the 1992 question about stablecoins: under what conditions does a price promise break? A model that answers that question has to be tested against something that actually happened. We test it against one event: the weekend of 10–13 March 2023, when USDC, the second-largest dollar stablecoin, fell to about 87 cents after its issuer disclosed that part of its reserves were stuck in a failed bank.

It is the right test case for three reasons.

1. **It is a real break of a real promise.** USDC is designed to be worth exactly one dollar. For about two days it was not.
2. **Almost everything is public.** The price on every venue, the token supply day by day, the issuer's statements, the reserve breakdown, the government's response. Few financial crises leave a record this complete.
3. **It recovered.** That lets us check not just how far the model falls but how and when it comes back.

It is also a very different event from 1992, and the differences matter. Black Wednesday was a speculative attack on a solvent government. March 2023 was a **bank run** on an issuer that turned out to be solvent but could not prove it, or pay out, until the banks reopened. Nobody attacked USDC. The section *Mapping March 2023 to the simulator* explains why the model can still use it.

---

## The short version

- USDC is issued by Circle. Each token is meant to be redeemable for one US dollar, and Circle holds reserves (mostly short-term US Treasury bills, plus cash in banks) to back it. In early March 2023 there were about $43 billion of USDC.
- On **Friday 10 March** California regulators closed Silicon Valley Bank (SVB). That night, at about 10:30 pm Eastern, Circle said that **$3.3 billion of its ~$40 billion of reserves** were still at SVB.
- Holders rushed to get out. Banks were closed for the weekend, so Circle could barely pay dollars out, and exchanges paused conversions. Most people who wanted out had to sell on the market. By about **2 am Saturday** USDC traded at about **$0.87**.
- On **Sunday 12 March at 6:15 pm** the Treasury, the Federal Reserve and the FDIC announced that all SVB depositors would be paid in full. USDC jumped back toward a dollar within hours.
- On **Monday 13 March** banks reopened, Circle resumed redemptions, and the price returned to par.
- Between 10 and 13 March the supply of USDC fell by about **$2.7 billion**. It kept falling for months.

## 1. The promise: how USDC works

A fiat-backed stablecoin is a simple promise: *give us a dollar and we give you a token; give us the token back and we give you a dollar.* The issuer creates (mints) tokens when dollars come in and destroys (burns) them when dollars go out.

Two things keep the market price near one dollar.

- **Redemption.** If USDC trades at 98 cents on an exchange, anyone with access to Circle can buy it there and redeem it for a dollar, pocketing two cents. That buying pushes the price back up. The same works in reverse above a dollar.
- **Belief.** Most holders cannot redeem directly; only Circle's approved customers can. Everyone else relies on the market price staying near a dollar because they believe redemption works.

The promise is only as good as the reserves behind it and the plumbing that moves them. In March 2023 Circle's reserves were reported as about **$32.4 billion in short-dated US Treasury bills (77%) and $9.7 billion in cash (23%)** held at banks. [1] The cash is the part that pays redemptions day to day. It sat at a small number of banks, and some of them were in trouble.

## 2. Why the promise became hard to keep

USDC's problem in March 2023 was not that the reserves were bad. It was that they were held at banks, and the banks that served crypto failed one after another in five days.

**Wednesday 8 March.** Silvergate, a bank used by much of the crypto industry, announced it would wind down and liquidate. [2] Circle had already cut ties with it the week before.

**Thursday 9 March.** SVB's share price fell about 60% after it disclosed, the evening before, losses on its bond portfolio and a plan to raise capital. [11] Depositors, many of them technology startups, began pulling money out. Circle started wiring its balances out of SVB that day. [3]

**Friday 10 March.** California's Department of Financial Protection and Innovation closed SVB and appointed the FDIC as receiver. [4] SVB had about $175 billion in deposits, most of them uninsured because they were above the $250,000 limit. Circle's Thursday wires had not gone through. Its $3.3 billion was now an uninsured deposit at a failed bank, with no way of knowing how much would be recovered or when.

**Sunday 12 March.** New York regulators closed Signature Bank, the other main bank for crypto firms, whose payment network Circle used to mint and redeem. [5]

So by Sunday all three banks the crypto industry relied on most were gone: Silvergate winding down, SVB and Signature closed. Circle's promise depended on a banking system that was coming apart underneath it.

## 3. The weekend

Times are US Eastern. Clocks went forward an hour at 2 am on Sunday 12 March, so Friday and Saturday times are EST (UTC−5) and Sunday and Monday times are EDT (UTC−4). Prices are Bitstamp USDC/USD hourly closes, committed in the repo. [6]

| When (ET) | What happened | USDC price |
|---|---|---|
| Fri 10 Mar, daytime | SVB closed by regulators. [4] | $1.000 |
| Fri 10 Mar, ~7 pm | Price starts to slip. | $0.997 |
| **Fri 10 Mar, 10:26 pm** | **Circle: "$3.3 billion of the ~$40 billion of USDC reserves remain at SVB."** [3] | $0.997 |
| Fri–Sat overnight | Coinbase pauses USD↔USDC conversion for the weekend; Binance suspends auto-conversion of USDC. [7] Outflows of USDC from centralised exchanges peak at about **$1.2 billion an hour**. [8] | falling |
| **Sat 11 Mar, ~2 am** | **Low.** Chainalysis: USDC "fell to $0.87" by 2 am. [8] Lowest hourly close in our series: $0.86267. | **$0.863** |
| Sat 11 Mar, morning | DAI, a stablecoin partly backed by USDC, falls with it, as low as $0.88. [7] Buyers start to step in. | $0.88–0.90 |
| Sat 11 Mar, afternoon–evening | Steady recovery as buyers bet on Circle being made whole. | $0.92–0.96 |
| Sat 11 Mar, ~10 pm | Brief rally. | $0.985 |
| Sun 12 Mar, daytime | Back down; the market waits for news. Signature Bank closed. [5] | $0.955–0.97 |
| **Sun 12 Mar, 6:15 pm EDT** | **Treasury, Fed and FDIC joint statement: SVB depositors "will have access to all of their money starting Monday, March 13."** [9] | $0.97 |
| Sun 12 Mar, ~8 pm | Circle CEO Jeremy Allaire: "100% of USDC reserves are also safe and secure"; settlements through BNY Mellon; operations resume when banks open. [10] | **$0.995** |
| **Mon 13 Mar, 9 am** | Banks reopen. Circle resumes redemptions. | $0.990 |
| Mon 13 Mar, ~11 am onward | Price back within a fraction of a cent of par. | $0.999 |

Two features of the path matter for the model.

- **The fall was fast; the recovery was slow.** USDC went from $0.997 to $0.863 in about four hours. It took the rest of the weekend, and a government announcement, to get back.
- **The price sat at a discount for two days.** From Saturday morning to Sunday evening it hovered between about 92 and 97 cents. Somebody was buying at those prices and holding.

## 4. Who sold, and who bought

**The sellers.** There was no single attacker. The selling came from many holders who did not want to be the last one holding a token whose backing might be 8% short: trading firms, DeFi protocols, funds, individuals. Most could not redeem with Circle over the weekend, so they sold on exchanges and on-chain pools like Curve and Uniswap, mainly into USDT and DAI. [8] Arbitrage and trading bots ran at record volume; one study counts about $4 billion of arbitrage volume on 11 March alone. [11]

**The buyers.** For the price to stop at 87 cents and climb back, someone had to buy. The obvious buyers were traders who believed Circle would eventually pay a dollar, whether because SVB's assets would cover most deposits, because Circle would make up a shortfall, or because the government would step in. Each of them was making a bet: buy at 90 cents, redeem for a dollar on Monday. We cannot see who they were from public data. We can see that they existed, because the price stopped falling while the selling was still going on.

**The issuer.** Circle did not buy USDC on the market to defend the price. Its only lever was redemption, and over the weekend redemption was mostly closed.

**The government.** The Sunday announcement was what ended the run. It did not act on USDC. It made Circle's deposit whole, and the market repriced the token within hours.

## 5. The cost

- **To Circle:** none of the $3.3 billion was lost. But the supply of USDC fell from about **$43.2 billion on 10 March to $40.5 billion on 13 March** and **$35.9 billion on 20 March** (DefiLlama daily data, committed in the repo). [12] Every one of those tokens was a customer leaving.
- **To holders who sold at the bottom:** up to 13 cents on the dollar. One trader lost almost $2 million in a single swap about 20 minutes after Circle's announcement. [11]
- **To DeFi:** protocols that used USDC as collateral or as a pool asset were hit at once. DAI, then about a third backed by USDC, lost its own peg with it; its USDC facility had hit its cap of 3.1 billion USDC, so no more DAI could be created against USDC. [7]
- **To buyers at the discount:** a gain of up to 13 cents per token, for those who held through Monday.

## 6. Aftermath

USDC's peg held after 13 March. Its market share did not. USDC's supply, which had peaked around $55 billion in mid-2022, was about **$26.7 billion by late July 2023**. [13] Analysts at the time put the loss down to a mix of the depeg's lasting damage to trust, US regulatory pressure on crypto, and competition from Tether. [13] Circle moved its settlement to BNY Mellon the night the guarantee was announced. [10]

For the industry, the lesson was blunt: a fully backed stablecoin is still a claim on a bank account, and the bank can fail. A promise can be solvent and still not be keepable on a weekend.

## How it is usually explained

**A bank run.** The classic model of a bank run (Diamond and Dybvig, 1983) says a bank that is perfectly sound can still fail if enough depositors believe others will withdraw first. Each depositor is rational to run if they think others will. USDC's weekend fits that pattern: Circle was not insolvent, but holders could not know that, and the cost of being last was high.

**Liquidity, not solvency.** The reserves existed. They could not be moved. The weekend's binding constraint was not how much money Circle had but how fast it could pay it out, and over a weekend with banks closed the answer was "very slowly." This is the same distinction the simulator draws between running out of reserves and running out of time (the note's §4).

**A lender of last resort.** In traditional finance a central bank stops a run on a sound bank by lending against its assets. Stablecoins have no such lender. In March 2023 the backstop came indirectly: the government protected the *bank's* depositors, and Circle was one of them. That backstop sits entirely outside the stablecoin.

## Mapping March 2023 to the simulator

The replay scenario is `scenarios/usdc-2023.yaml` (and `usdc-2023-tranches.yaml` for the ladder of believers). Every parameter's source is in [`calibration/SOURCES.md`](calibration/SOURCES.md); the replay and its results are in [`calibration/VALIDATION.md`](calibration/VALIDATION.md).

| March 2023 | Simulator | Value |
|---|---|---|
| The run: holders selling USDC, all of them added up | **Attacker** | 11,540,596 model units = the net fall in USDC supply 10 → 13 March, **$2.71B** [12] |
| When the run started | Attacker `start_step` | Circle's disclosure, Fri 22:00 ET = step 8,100 (27.0 h into the series) |
| How fast holders sold | Attacker `pace` | 0.2% of the remaining stock every 12 seconds, fitted to the timing of the low |
| Circle not buying on the market | **Defender** | switched off |
| Circle's T-bill reserves | Redemption `reserves` | $32.4B [1] |
| Redemption over the weekend, banks closed | Redemption `capacity_per_step` | slow: about $142k every 12 s, the observed average burn rate |
| Banks reopen Monday 9 am | Redemption capacity change | fast from step 25,500 (85.0 h) |
| Traders buying at a discount, betting on par | **Holder** (the believer) | $2.15B at one entry price, or $2.54B spread across four (2/5/10/20%) |
| Arbitrage bots | **Arbitrageur** | buys below the reference by more than fees and redeems |
| All of USDC's market depth: Curve, Uniswap, exchanges, OTC | One **AMM pool** | depth fitted: about $3.9B a side, 16.7× Curve's USDC leg |
| USDC's price elsewhere | **Reference price** fed to the oracle | the Bitstamp hourly series itself |
| The government guarantee | *nothing* | the model has no government; it only has redemption speeding up on Monday |

**Why a bank run can stand in for an attack.** The model's attacker is a stock of tokens sold at a pace. It has no motive. A token sold in fear moves the pool's price exactly as a token sold for profit does. So the model can replay a run by sizing the attacker to the run's total net selling. What it cannot do is explain *why* the selling happened or when it would stop; those are inputs, not outputs.

**What the replay shows.** Without the believers the model fell to about 42 cents, four times too far. With one believer the low was 88.6 cents (−1,138 bps) against 86.3 observed, three hours late. With the believers' money spread over four entry prices the low was 86.8 cents (−1,323 bps) at 2:12 am against 86.3 at 2 am. Figures 1 and 1b in the note show both.

**What it cannot claim.**

- One event. A second would test whether the believers' size generalises.
- The reference price fed to the model *is* the observed USDC price, so part of the later weekend path is the input showing through. The trough itself is set by the attacker against the believers, not by the input (`VALIDATION.md` checks this).
- The weekend redemption rate is the average burn over 11–15 March, which includes Monday's catch-up. The real weekend channel was probably slower.
- Who the buyers were, and at what prices they bought, is not public. The ladder of entry prices is an assumption, stated as one.
- The model has no government. Its recovery comes from redemption reopening on Monday, which is also when it came in reality, but the cause in reality was the Sunday guarantee.

## 1992 and 2023 side by side

| | Black Wednesday, 1992 | USDC, March 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| The promise | £1 ≥ DM 2.78 (ERM floor) | 1 USDC = $1 (redemption) |
| Who made it | UK government | Circle, a private company |
| What broke it | Speculators betting the government would not keep paying the price of defence | Holders fearing the reserves were short |
| Was the defender solvent? | Yes, but unwilling to keep rates high in a recession | Yes, but unable to prove it or pay out over a weekend |
| Defence | Buy sterling with reserves; raise rates | None on the market; redemption mostly closed |
| Attackers | Quantum Fund (~$10B), banks, unwinding convergence trades | No attacker; a run by many holders |
| Buyers who held the line | The Bank of England | Traders betting on par |
| How it ended | The government gave up the peg at 7:40 pm | An outside authority guaranteed the bank deposit |
| Outcome | Peg broken; sterling left the ERM | Peg restored in ~60 hours; issuer lost ~40% of supply within five months |

The note's two-exits finding comes straight out of this table. In 1992 a holder who lost faith could only sell to the Bank, which drained the reserves. In 2023 a holder who lost faith mostly sold on the market, because redemption was closed, which crashed the price but left the reserves untouched. Different exit, different damage.
